How Balance Shogi came to be

I trained an AI for Fuseki Shogi and Sente won far too often. This is how I stopped chasing a magic number in the rules and let the players balance the game themselves.

1. The problem

Fuseki Shogi is a variant in which both sides place 20 pieces on an empty board, one at a time, before anything moves. Instead of a single starting position there are about 1037 of them. I wanted a shogi that does not feel researched to death, so I built an AI you can play in the browser: a network that places the pieces, trained by self-play, and YaneuraOu for everything from move 41 on.

As the network improved, one thing became impossible to ignore. Sente won too much. Across all promotion gates of the training run (games between equally strong policies, 10,767 in total) Sente scored 62.1%, and more training did not bring it down.

2. First-move advantage in ordinary shogi

This is not special to Fuseki Shogi. In professional human games Sente wins 52–53%, but the stronger the engines, the more Sente wins.

Within the same tournament the number rises from the qualifiers to the final. The advantage is a function of strength, and a time handicap did not close it.

3. Things I tried in the rules

My first instinct was to fix it in the text of the rules: restrict the first move to an edge pawn, forbid placements where pieces attack each other, reduce the number of ranks. Of everything I measured, the only change that helped was “place within three ranks, pawns on the third rank” — a geometry close to ordinary shogi — and even that still gave Sente 53.8% with random placement.

Then it clicked. Removing contact or changing the ranks does not touch the core of the advantage. What moves is the value of a tempo, and that depends on the strength of whoever is playing, not on the geometry. A rule that looks like 50% at one strength will be off at another.

4. A fixed rule cannot follow strength

Chess also favours White, but at the top the draw rate rises and absorbs the advantage. Shogi has pieces in hand and almost no draws. So even if I found a rule that measured 50% in my environment (YaneuraOu with an older evaluation), it would not hold for the newest engines. I stopped looking for a number.

Awkwardly, going deeper with the same engine pushed ordinary shogi down: Sente scored 54.0% at 200k nodes and 50.1% at 1M. That suggests the rising tournament figures owe more to opening books and modern evaluations than to depth alone. Either way, a rule calibrated here carries no guarantee over there.

5. Cut and choose

Only one mechanism is known to stay balanced regardless of strength: one player cuts, the other chooses. It is how two people share a cake, and it is standard in Hex and in Gomoku. Gomoku’s Swap2 opening — the first player puts down two black stones and one white, the second player picks a colour — brought the first player’s 67% down to about 52%.

It breaks, though, when the cutter is also the one who eats. If a player may choose a camp after the placement phase, they simply build their own camp badly and take the opponent’s. That is why I had rejected the pie rule earlier.

Fuseki Shogi, however, has a boundary between placing and moving, and that allows cutting, choosing and building to be separated.

CutAt the very start, one player places both kings, not knowing which side they will get.
ChooseThe other player looks at the two kings and takes Sente or Gote. Nothing has been built yet.
BuildThe remaining 19 pieces each are placed alternately, Sente first. This part is unchanged.

The cutter is neutral because they do not know their side; the chooser cannot sandbag because they have not built anything. Whatever advantage Sente still has is cancelled every game by the cutter putting the Sente king on a slightly worse square.

6. Why the kings

Is cutting with the kings alone enough? Do king squares carry enough value to cancel the first-move advantage? That can be measured. For every pair of squares (36 for the Sente king × 36 for the Gote king) I let the placement network finish the placement from that position and had YaneuraOu score the move-41 position — 128 rollouts per pair for the published table, about 14 minutes in total.

9.5%Sente’s score with the worst pair, 4f·2a (400 real games)
99.5%Sente’s score with the best pair, 5i·1d (400 real games)

The range is 95 percentage points — far more than the first-move advantage in this environment (about 12 points). Checked against real games (6 pairs × 400 games) the table was off by 2.2 points on average.

But the number of pairs in the band reflects our measurement precision, not the board. The band is defined as “|V − 0.5| within the error (2·SE)”, so halving the rollouts widens the error by about 1.4× and widens the band with it. The earlier table, built with 256 rollouts per pair, had 104 pairs in its band. The jump to 156 is not the game changing. That distinction matters in the next section.

A king on the front rank (closest to the opponent) loses a lot; a back-rank corner is best. This table is exactly what the AI’s placer and chooser consult.

7. Does it actually balance?

Games between AIs that both consult the same table, with YaneuraOu at 200k nodes from move 41 on both sides.

How the placer picksGamesSente winsPlacer wins
at random (36×36)80064.5%23.8% ± 1.5
uniformly from the 156-pair band4,00049.9%46.3% ± 0.8
from the 21 most balanced pairs4,20050.2%49.2% ± 0.8

A placer who does not think about the kings loses three games in four. The design goal — the only way for the placer not to lose out is to balance the kings — shows up directly in the numbers.

But drawing uniformly from the band scored only 46.3% over 4,000 games. That is 3.7 points to the chooser, 4.6 times the standard error: too large to be chance.

The cause was the width of the band. As the previous section notes, most of that width is our own measurement error — ±4.4 points in the published table. Draw uniformly from it and you hand over a pair that is tilted by 3 points on average, and the chooser only has to read the sign. What was unbalanced was neither the rule nor the geometry of the kings, but the way the placer drew. There is no reason for the placer to use room that measurement error opened up.

So the placer now draws from the 21 pairs inside the design tolerance (±1 point) rather than the whole 156-pair band. Over 4,200 games the placer scored 49.2% ± 0.8 — 1.0σ from the null point, no longer distinguishable. With 21 pairs across 11 classes, the kings still differ from game to game.

It is not perfect. The table calls all 21 of those pairs “50.0%”, yet real games spread them from 43.0% to 56.5%. Subtracting sampling error leaves about 1.6 points of genuine error in the table. Part of why this looks balanced is that the placer and the chooser read the same table. A chooser with a better table could exploit that residue. The precision of the table is the ceiling on fairness.

8. The name

One player places both kings, the other takes whichever they prefer. On one pan of the scale sits the first move, on the other the safety of the kings, and the placer has no choice but to balance them. Hence Balance Shogi — in Japanese tenbin shōgi (天秤将棋), “scale shogi”.

9. What comes next

  • The placement network has now been retrained under Balance Shogi. Around a hundred generations made the policy clearly stronger, yet the width of the balanced band barely moved: 90.7 → 86.9 points. The structure of the balance does not seem to follow the strength of the policy. The table and the network are still updated as a pair.
  • Making the table more precise. Most of the band’s width comes from the rollout count, and so does the ceiling on fairness (about 1.6 points).
  • The newest engines have a much larger first-move advantage. If the kings alone stop being enough, the cut widens to “king and rook”.
  • A third option in the style of Gomoku’s Swap2: instead of choosing, add one piece to each camp and hand the choice back. Then a cutter cannot impose a placement only they have studied.
